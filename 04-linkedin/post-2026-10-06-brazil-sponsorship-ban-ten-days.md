## Testo

Brazil gave its football clubs ten days to take betting sponsors off their shirts.

Provisional Measure 1.394, published on 25 September, required advertising material and sponsorship signs to be removed within ten days, with an exception for incidental advertising in content published before the measure. That window closed yesterday.

For Flamengo alone, the betting shirt deal is worth R$268.5 million a season, close to 13% of the club's 2025 gross revenue. The club has gone to the Supreme Court. Even before the measure, twenty-two clubs had warned of "the end of Brazilian football" in a joint statement. Once it was published, the government called off a meeting with them scheduled for 29 September, after the clubs signalled they would not attend.

I have written about sponsorship bans from the Italian side before. Put Brazil next to Italy and Belgium, and two differences show.

The first is time. Italy allowed advertising contracts already in force on 14 July 2018 to run until they expired, and no later than a year after that date. Belgium adopted its restrictions in 2023: since 2025 shirt logos have been limited in size and kept off the front, and sponsorship of professional clubs ends on 1 January 2028. Brazil gave ten days.

The second is what was banned. Italy restricted advertising while licensed gambling itself remained legal. Brazil's measure prohibits the underlying activity, fixed-odds betting, including sports betting and online casino games, while other authorised lotteries remain.

A longer transition gives clubs time to replace sponsorship income and revise spending already committed. Ten days leave very little of that time.

Congress has until 23 November, subject to extension, to convert the measure into law, and it may amend it. The parliamentary timetable does not extend the removal deadline.

If your business relies on sport sponsorship in a regulated market, find the transition clause in your country's rules before you need it.
