## Testo

Brazil gave its football clubs ten days to take betting sponsors off their shirts.

Provisional Measure 1.394, signed on 25 September, set that window for advertising and sponsorship to come down. It closed yesterday.

For Flamengo alone, the betting shirt deal is worth R$268.5 million a season, close to 13% of the club's 2025 gross revenue. The club has gone to the Supreme Court. Twenty-two clubs signed a joint statement under the line "The end of Brazilian football", and on 29 September the government called off a meeting with them after the clubs signalled they would not attend.

I have written about sponsorship bans from the Italian side before. Put Brazil next to Italy and Belgium, and two differences show.

The first is time. When Italy banned gambling advertising in 2018, contracts already running could continue until they expired, for a year at most. Belgium set its shirt sponsorship ban in 2023, and it takes full effect on 1 January 2028. Brazil gave ten days.

The second is what was banned. Italy prohibits advertising a product that stays legal, and clubs found a narrow route through news-style brands built around odds information. Brazil bans the product itself, so there is no version of the brand left to put on a shirt.

A ban with a runway is a policy clubs can plan around. A ban with ten days' notice lands on budgets that were already written.

Congress has until 23 November, extendable, to convert the measure, and it can amend it. Until then, the ten days are the rule.

If your business relies on sport sponsorship in a regulated market, find the transition clause in your country's rules before you need it.
