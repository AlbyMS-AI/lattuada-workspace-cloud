Italy's regulator moved a compliance deadline by six months, from 13 May to 13 November. Not out of leniency: February monitoring showed operators were not going to make it.

A whole market, late on one infrastructure upgrade. That is what integration work costs, written into a regulator's calendar instead of a quote.

Three costs sit outside the contract you sign.

The first is the exit nobody prices. Operators rarely replace a provider that is already integrated and running. They open up when they lack a title, or when the rate is bad. At equal pricing, what is in stays in. So the number you negotiate on day one becomes your baseline for years, not because the contract locks you, but because switching costs more than the saving.

The second is reconciliation. Where does the player balance live? A seamless model keeps one balance and writes to it in real time. A transfer model keeps one per product and moves funds in batches. Every direct integration built on a different architecture adds another surface where the numbers have to agree, at a different hour, with its own edge cases.

The third follows from the second, and it is the one that reaches your licence. Deposit limits, loss limits and self-exclusion hold on a single balance. Across separate balances a player can move funds between products and route around them. Regulators look at that distinction.

Ask for the wallet model before you ask for the rate. Then ask what it takes to leave.
